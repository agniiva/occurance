You've done it. Stared at a fire for way too long. Watched water move over rocks until your sense of time dissolved. Stood somewhere windy and felt something pull at you that had nothing to do with air. There's a reason we can't look away from these things. I think I finally know what it is.

---

Chaos isn't randomness. That's the first thing people get wrong.

A chaotic system follows rules. Strict ones, actually. But the smallest difference in where you start creates a completely different outcome. Two leaves fall into a stream an inch apart. One ends up pinned against a rock. The other makes it to the ocean.

Same river. Same physics. Completely different lives.

I've been that leaf. More than once.

---

I grew up in Jhargram. Tier-3 village in West Bengal. The kind of place where the trajectory is mostly written for you. Study hard. Crack the exam. Get the job. Settle.

I didn't crack the exam.

What followed was Kolkata. Two, three years of doing five things at once and none of them working. Freelancing. Projects that went nowhere. A fog that I didn't recognize as depression until I was already deep in it. I had no identity. Not a student anymore. Not a professional yet. Not anything, really.

Then I moved to Bangalore with almost nothing. Met Ben van Sprundel. We built BenAI together. I worked with Greg Isenberg on Boring Marketing. Things started to take shape.

But here's what I keep coming back to. The shape didn't emerge from the stable periods. It emerged from the spaces between them.

---

There's a word for those spaces. Liminality.

It comes from the Latin "limen," meaning threshold. The space between one room and the next. You're not where you were. You're not where you're going. You're in the doorway.

Between Kolkata and Bangalore. Between freelancer and operator. Between "this is never going to work" and "wait, maybe it is." Between zero and something.

Those transitions feel terrible. You have no ground under you. No label. No title. No clean answer to "so what do you do?" You can't point to anything solid and say, "this is me."

That's the liminal space.

And that's exactly where chaos lives.

---

When your life is stable, you're on a track. Momentum carries you. Your decisions matter, sure, but the range of possible outcomes is narrow. You're a boulder rolling downhill. The path is mostly set.

But in the liminal phase, you're not a boulder. You're a leaf in a stream. Everything is sensitive. The smallest input changes the entire trajectory.

Most people hate this. Understandably.

So they try to skip it. They want to go from A to B directly. Stable job to stable job. Known identity to known identity. The in-between makes them panic, and they grab the first thing that looks like solid ground.

Take the safe offer. Pick the obvious path. Collapse the uncertainty as fast as possible.

That's why most people never really change. Not because they can't. Because they won't stay in the liminal space long enough for chaos to do its work.

---

The thing nobody tells you about the in-between is that it's the only time small moves actually matter.

When I was in Kolkata, stuck, identity-less, nothing working. One random conversation led to Bangalore. Not a grand plan. A conversation. If I'd had that conversation a week earlier or a week later, with slightly different words, maybe I'd still be in Kolkata. Maybe I'd be somewhere else entirely.

One "yes" to something that seemed weird at the time opened a door I didn't know existed. One person I met at exactly the right moment became the person I'd build a company with.

This is what chaos theory actually means for your life. Not that everything is random. That in the transitional moments, you are maximally sensitive to small inputs. A single decision, a single meeting, a single uncomfortable "yes" can redirect everything.

This doesn't happen when things are stable. It happens when things are uncertain.

So the worst thing you can do in a liminal phase is freeze. Or grab the first exit. The best thing you can do is stay open. Move. Say yes to things that don't make sense yet. Talk to people who aren't in your current world. The moves feel small. They're not.

---

Here's what's strange, though.

If you'd looked at my life from outside during those years, it would have looked like pure chaos. Trying everything. Failing at most of it. No coherent narrative. No clear direction.

But there was a pattern. I just couldn't see it from inside.

I kept orbiting the same things. Building. Operating. Solving problems for real people. Every failed project, every dead-end freelance gig, every "what am I even doing" moment. They all circled the same center of gravity.

In chaos theory, they call this a strange attractor. The system looks random, but it keeps being pulled toward a shape. You can't predict any single point on the trajectory. But zoom out, and the shape is unmistakable.

The attractor was always there. I just couldn't see it while I was in the doorway. You never can. You only see the pattern after.

---

The fire. The water. The wind.

Maybe we stare at them because they're mirrors. They're showing us what our lives actually look like from the inside. Constant transition. Constant sensitivity to small changes. Constant movement that somehow, over time, holds a shape.

We keep waiting for the chaos to end. For things to settle. For the stable state where we finally know who we are and what we're doing.

I don't think it works that way.

The liminal space isn't the gap between your real life. It is your real life. The transitions aren't interruptions to the story. They're the whole story.

Chaos isn't the enemy. It's the operating system.

And right now, wherever you are, if the ground feels uncertain and you don't know what comes next. Good. You're in the doorway. That's where everything changes.

Stay there a little longer than feels comfortable.
